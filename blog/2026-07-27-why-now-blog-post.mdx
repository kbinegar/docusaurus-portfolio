# Why Become a Technical Writer in the Age of AI?
#### An Inspection of AI Audiences and Writing 

Being a newcomer to any field in the age of AI presents a few unique 
questions to young professionals. In the world of technical writing, these questions include
considerations of both AI as a primary audience, and AI capabilities within authorship.

{/* truncate */}

As we continue to watch Language Learning Models (LLMs), or AI “agents,” absorb larger and larger quantities 
of labor expectations, many pre-established technical writing professionals have begun considering the security 
of writing roles. I saw this myself at the 2026 Write the Docs (WTD) Conference in Portland, Oregon. Hosted in May, 
several, if not the majority of, discussions revolved around AI — with some notable figures asking: 
“When is it time to stop writing for humans, and begin writing solely for agents?”

As a relative newcomer to the technical writing field, I must admit this question initially felt quite jarring. 
It was not that I couldn't understand or see the impact and use of AI, but more so that in my previous roles, the 
act of creation was seen as one done solely by and for other humans — technical in nature or not. 

Existentially, my arguments towards centering technical writing, communications, and the study of information 
structuring as philosophies worth building my life around ran on the assumption that this labor existed both 
because of, and for human benefit. As someone who left the study of astrophysics due to a missing “human” 
component, I know that this concept matters to me. 

I reasoned that even in the face of creating documentation solely for agents, the resultant impact is still
in favor of improving the human user or developer experience. My belief in participating in communication 
for the sake of improving human experiences can be maintained in the wake of writing for agents so long as 
it can be assumed that functionality exists as an imperative _because of_ user demand, needs, and experiences. 

What functionality looks like is bound to change in any field, and technical writers are actively adapting 
to create documentation that serves LLMs. An ever shifting audience is an ever shifting form of 
study. This is what I excel in — having researched how information design impacts different types of audiences, as the recipient of a 
BA and MA in Communications with emphases in the audience experience, and as a writing 
consultant who has worked with hundreds of writers across disciplines.

Ah. I could breathe again. 

However, there still remains the subject of authorship. The day before considering agents as audience, another 
question had been asked at WTD. A senior technical writer with over 30 years of experience inquired: “If 
agents are taking entry-level technical writing roles, what are the new classes of technical communication students 
and young professionals supposed to do?” A simple enough question, one many fields are asking, 
but one that fewer are answering. I approached the documentarian after the meeting and offered my own answer: 

> I use LLMs to teach me the job they took. Because of this, I was able to use my time in formal education to 
learn sociological functionality skills that AI cannot yet replicate: human connection, storytelling, 
identity, and what it means to understand an audience and understand what builds comprehension.  
<br></br>

I am getting to dive into a field with a unique advantage: I am not shifting from traditional ways into 
new technologies, I am actively learning modern workflows with the new technologies this field is directly 
looking to integrate.

It was very easy to ask ChatGPT or Claude AI to create a list of programs, languages, and platforms that 
I needed to grow proficient in to break into this field, and easier still to prompt them to produce a 
syllabus for each task on my road to becoming a technical writer. It is not very easy to provide LLMs 
with:

*   A cultural identity formed both in and out of community. 
*   An awareness of how identity alters communication structures, assumptions, and deliveries.
*   The ability to study the nuance of human expression and information architecture by examining their 
expressive products. 
*   The experience of being challenged by unique thoughts and arguments presented by peers — sculpted 
by their own insular experiences and identities.

Spending two degrees in two countries dedicated to the study of communication, psychology, neurology, 
pedagogy, history, and sociology has allowed me to grow into a communicator of many kinds. Mostly, I have an acute 
awareness of humanity in ways I simply do not believe AI will ever possess. If it is only hubris that allows 
me to think myself superior to AI's audience awareness abilities, at least it is a flaw within me that 
can only be found in man.

LLMs can express understanding about why a human grieves, an agent can draft emails to a boss to request bereavement, 
and, yes, AI can even provide emotional advice based off of a user's cultural background. But ultimately, an AI 
agent will never feel that thin spiral of electricity you will feel behind your throat, and down your spine 
when a stranger in the airport looks like your friend whose English papers you used to edit, before he 
died on an unassuming summer day seven years prior. 

So, to answer the question “Why become a technical writer now?” — in the midst of AI autonomy growing 
in both the authorship of, and use of, documentation — I say because tools are just tools.

LLMs are a huge, _structural_ leap in tool functionality that you and I get to witness and wield. But so long as there 
are human users or human creators, it is we who will continue to drive the needs 
of communication. I am grateful to be able to access these tools to guide my skill development, but I 
am gratified to have spent my time in formal education studying the ways in which we learn, 
communicate, and develop. Above all else, I am strengthened by my human experience, 
and I am wholly lucky to have spent part of my one precious life editing my friend's papers, before 
there was nothing left of him with which to communicate.
