A case for maintaining humanity alongside LLMs

Why Become a Technical Writer in the Age of AI?
an inspection of ai audiences and writing 

As we continue to watch Language Learning Models (LLMs), or AI “agents,” absorb larger and larger quantities 
of labor expectations, many pre-established technical writing professionals have begun considering the security 
of writing roles. I saw this myself at the 2026 Write the Docs (WTD) Conference in Portland, Oregon. Hosted in May, 
several, if not the majority of, discussions revolved around AI—with some notable figures asking the question: 
“When is it time to stop writing for humans, and begin writing solely for agents?”

As a relative newcomer to the technical writing field, I must admit this question initially felt quite jarring. 
It is not that I cannot understand or see the impact and use of AI, but more so that in my previous roles, the 
act of creation was seen as one done solely by and for other humans —technical in nature or not. 

Existentially, my arguments towards centering technical writing, communications, and the study of information 
structuring as philosophies worth building my life around ran on the assumption that this labor existed both 
because of and for human benefit. As someone who left the study of astrophysics due to a missing “human” 
component, I know this concept matters to me. 

I reason that even in the face of creating documentation solely for agents, the resultant impact is still
in favor of improving the human user or developer experience. My belief in participating in communication 
for the sake of improving human experiences can be maintained in the wake of writing for agents so long as 
it can be assumed that functionality exists as an imperative because of user demand, needs, and experiences. 

What functionality looks like is bound to change in any field, and technical writers are actively adapting 
to create documentation that functionally serves LLMs. An ever shifting audience is an ever shifting form of 
study. This is what I excel in. 

Ah. I can breathe again. 

But there is still the question not of audience, but of authorship. As LLM tools grow, can their communication 
and drafting abilities fill the role of a technical writer with as much proficiency? 

My answer, as you might have guessed, is “No.”

The day before the question of writing for LLMs was posed, another question was asked at WTD. A senior 
technical writer with over 30 years of experience inquired: “If agents are taking entry-level technical 
writing roles, what are the new classes of technical students and young professionals supposed to do?” A 
simple enough question, one many fields are asking, but one that fewer are answering. I approached the 
documentarian after the meeting and offered my own answer: 

I use LLMs to teach me the job they took. Because of this, I was able to use my time in formal education to 
learn sociological functionality skills that AI cannot yet replicate: human connection, storytelling, 
identity, and what it means to understand an audience and understand what builds comprehension.

I am getting to dive into a field with a unique advantage: I am not shifting from traditional ways into 
new technologies, I am actively learning modern workflows with the new technologies this field is directly 
looking to integrate.

It was very easy to ask ChatGPT or ClaudeAI to create a list of programs, languages, and platforms that 
I needed to grow proficient in to break into this field, and easier still to prompt them to produce a 
syllabus for each task on my road to becoming a technical writer. It is not very easy to provide LLMs 
with:

A cultural identity formed both in and out of community. 
An awareness of how identity alters communication structures, assumptions, and deliveries.
The ability to study the nuance of human expression and information architecture by examining their 
expressive products. 
The experience of being challenged by unique thoughts and arguments by peers — sculpted by their own 
insular experiences and identities.

Spending two degrees in two countries dedicated to the study of human communication, psychology, neurology, 
pedagogy, history, and sociology has grown me into a communicator of many kinds. Mostly, I have an acute 
awareness of humanity in ways I simply do not believe AI will ever possess. If it is only hubris that allows 
me to think my capabilities superior to AI’s audience awareness, at least it is something within me that 
can only be found in man.
- that only man can posses? (double use of possess) 

LLMs can express understanding about why a human grieves, an agent can draft emails to a boss to request bereavement, 
and, yes, AI can even provide advice based off of users’ cultural backgrounds. But, ultimately, an AI agent will 
never feel that thin spiral of electricity you will feel behind your throat, and down your spine when a stranger 
in the airport looks like your friend whose english papers you used to edit, before he died on a summer day 
seven years prior. 

So, to answer the question “Why become a technical writer now?” — in the midst of AI autonomy growing 
in both the authorship of, and the use of, documentation — I say because tools are just tools.

LLMs are a ginormous leap in tool functionality that you and I get to witness. But so long as there 
are human users or human creators, it is humans us? who will continue to drive the functionality needs 
of communication. I am lucky enough to have spent my time in formal education studying the ways in 
which we learn, communicate, and develop; but mostly, I am lucky enough to have spent part of my one 
precious life editing my friend’s papers, before there was nothing left of him with which to communicate.

Like many, stumbling upon the WTD website felt like finding gold up in a bank of a thriving river of resources.
Rewinding several months to March of 2026, I was a freshly primed and newly motivated learner, waiting 
to dive into the river’s waters. With such a wide variety of online resources, between podcasts and portfolios, 
the WTD website kindly handed me a lifejacket. I jumped in, using AI myself to help guide my path.

Agents are surely fed data and information on these topics, yes. However, I believe we are not at a point 
where an agent can understand much of, let alone replicate, genuine human interaction and understanding. 
I don’t believe this to be novel statement. However, it helps elucidate that the same concept that draws me 
to communications is what draws me to technical writing, even if for an agent: audience connection, understanding, and analysis.
